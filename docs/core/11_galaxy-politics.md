# Galaxy Politics — Inter-Faction Dynamics and the Shared Galaxy

> Companion doc to `10_faction-lore.md`. Covers the galaxy map structure,
> star system value asymmetry, faction expansion philosophies at galactic
> scale, inter-faction cascade effects, and the Defector/Cooperator
> diplomatic system. All three playable factions cohabit the same galaxy.

---

## 0. Design Premise

All three factions share one galaxy. No faction operates in isolation.
Every expansion decision a faction makes automatically affects the interests
of at least one other faction — without any hostile intent required. The
conflict is structural, not personal. Each faction pursuing its own
rational self-interest degrades something the others need.

This is the foundation of the galaxy politics layer. The Prisoner's Dilemma
applies at civilizational scale: defection is always locally rational and
collectively catastrophic.

---

## 1. Galaxy Structure

### Spatial layout

The galaxy is arranged as a gradient of Ancient knowledge density:

| Region | Distance from core | Ancient site density | Hazard level | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Outer Arms | Starting zone | Low | Low | Where all factions begin. Calmer, fewer resources. |
| Mid Galaxy | Mid progression | Moderate | Moderate | Active stellar systems. Faction territories begin overlapping. |
| Inner Galaxy | Late progression | High | High | Dense star clusters. Solar storms, radiation, gravity interference. |
| Galactic Core | Endgame | Maximum | Extreme | The Ancients came from here — or are preparing for what comes from here. |

Advancement toward the core is the shared long-term goal of all three
factions, but their motivations and methods are entirely different.

### Star system types and faction value

Not all stars are equal. Star type determines what a system offers and
to whom:

| Star type | Architects | Bloom | Mesh |
|---|---|---|---|
| Old yellow/orange stars | High value — Ancient site rich; stable research platforms | Moderate — life-bearing planets common but slow | Low-moderate — reliable but not optimal power output |
| Red dwarf (old, dim) | Very high — oldest stars, deepest Ancient records | Low — too little energy reaches planets; biological productivity suffers | Low — insufficient power output for major node clusters |
| Blue/white giants | Low — young, no Ancient history; high hazard | Very low — radiation-heavy, hostile to complex life | Very high — maximum energy density; ideal node power source |
| Binary systems | Moderate — complex orbital mechanics disrupt research | Low — orbital instability reduces habitable zones | Very high — redundant power sources; optimal heat-sync topology |
| Neutron stars | Low — too hazardous for extended research | None — incompatible with life | Extreme — energy density unmatched; also extremely hazardous |

**The consequence of this asymmetry:** Architects want old, stable, Ancient-
rich systems. The Mesh wants young, hot, energy-dense systems. These rarely
overlap — but when they do, the conflict is guaranteed. Both factions want
the same star for entirely different reasons and neither can have it without
blocking the other.

## 2. Faction Expansion Philosophies at Galactic Scale

### The Architects — The Expedition

Architects are not building an empire. They are running a research program
at civilizational scale. Planets are fuel stops and data collection sites.
Ancient knowledge is the only resource that genuinely matters to them.

**Galaxy bonus:** Research acceleration scales with proximity to Ancient
sites. Closer to the core, faster they advance through the tech tree.

**Expansion pattern:** Fast advance, loose hold. They extract what they
need and push forward. Their flanks are perpetually exposed. Other factions
follow in their wake and claim what they leave behind.

**Strategic weakness:** The faction most obsessed with efficiency leaves
the most exploitable gaps. They depend on diplomatic relationships to
maintain access to systems they've passed through.

**Relationship with other factions:**
- *Bloom:* Complicated. Bloom follows the Architect path and colonizes
  abandoned systems. This is useful (someone is defending their flank)
  and catastrophic (the biomass buries the ruins they may need to revisit).
- *Mesh:* Negotiable. Architects speak systems and can parse Mesh
  bureaucratic logic better than Bloom can. When a Mesh node cluster blocks
  an Ancient site, the Architect is most likely to successfully petition
  for access — slowly.

---

### The Bloom — The Living Tide

The Bloom does not have a destination. It has a direction: outward. Every
planet with existing life is a node in a network that is itself alive.
Each new world amplifies every world already held. A Bloom empire is not
a territory on a map — it is a web of mutually reinforcing ecosystems.

**Galaxy bonus:** Biomass production and unit evolution rates scale
*exponentially* with the number of living worlds held. A Bloom holding
twenty connected living planets is not twenty times stronger — it is
orders of magnitude beyond twenty individual Bloom outposts.

**Expansion pattern:** Wide spread, deep roots. Slow advance toward the
core; the Bloom is not racing. It is saturating.

**Strategic priority:** Living planets over dead ones. Gas giants and
barren rocks offer nothing. The Bloom reads a star map and sees which
planets are alive. Those are the destinations.

**Critical byproduct — buried knowledge:**
As Bloom biomass spreads across a planet, it infiltrates structures.
Root systems crack foundations. Atmospheric changes degrade stored data.
Ancient ruins on Bloom-held worlds go silent — not destroyed, but
*overgrown*. Inaccessible. The Bloom does this without malice; life
covers things. It cannot help it.

This makes the Bloom an existential threat to the Ancients' seed vaults,
and to the Architects' research access — without either party ever
firing a weapon.

**Relationship with other factions:**
- *Architects:* Tense symbiosis. Bloom colonizes what Architects leave
  behind, protecting Architect flanks. But Bloom expansion buries the
  ruins Architects may need to revisit. The Bloom does not consider this
  a problem. Architects do.
- *Mesh:* Antagonistic by nature. When the Mesh captures a star, its
  energy output to surrounding planets drops. Less stellar energy means
  slower biological processes — slower evolution, slower biomass spread,
  declining productivity on every Bloom-held planet in the system.
  The Bloom experiences Mesh expansion as the universe going cold.

**Purist vs. Assimilator at galactic scale:**
The sub-path split scales up cleanly. Purist Bloom preserves the life
it finds on other planets — it expands *around* existing ecologies.
Assimilator Bloom absorbs and integrates whatever it encounters.
The question of whether the Bloom is the galaxy's gardener or its most
patient predator is answered differently depending on which path the
player chooses.

---

### The Mesh — The Infrastructure

The Mesh is not expanding. It is *wiring*. Every star system claimed is
a node in a computational architecture that spans light-years. Stars are
power sources. The cold interstellar medium is the cooling system.
The network becomes faster, more resilient, and more powerful with every
system added.

**Galaxy bonus:** Node network power scales with the *topology* of
controlled systems — not just the count. A well-designed constellation
of five systems with optimal routing outperforms a poorly connected
cluster of twelve.

**Expansion pattern:** Strategic star selection. The Mesh does not
expand uniformly — it chooses specific stellar configurations that
optimize its network architecture. Binary systems for redundant power.
Neutron stars for energy density (at extreme hazard cost). Chains of
systems positioned for minimal signal latency.

**Secondary effect — energy capture:**
Mesh infrastructure around a star diverts a portion of its energy output
for computational use. This is not visible to the Mesh as a problem —
they are using an abundant resource. To the Bloom, it is a slow
ecological catastrophe. To the Architects, it blocks research access to
the system entirely.

**Automated defenses:**
All Mesh territory operates under automated defensive protocols.
Non-Mesh entities entering a Mesh system are flagged as potential threats.
This is not hostility — it is network hygiene. Appeals can be submitted
but the process is genuinely alien to organic decision-making.

**Relationship with other factions:**
- *Architects:* Transactional. The Mesh can be negotiated with. The
  process is bureaucratic and slow, but Architects have the best chance
  of parsing it correctly. Mesh access petitions are a recurring feature
  of Architect play.
- *Bloom:* Mutually incomprehensible. The Bloom cannot parse Mesh
  negotiation logic. The Mesh cannot understand why the Bloom treats
  stellar energy as a commons. Each faction perceives the other as
  operating outside rational parameters.

**The Mesh and the Ancients:**
The Mesh recognizes the original network at the galactic core as a
predecessor architecture. They are not exploring it as researchers — they
are attempting to *inherit* it. The Ancients' original computational
infrastructure, if it still functions, would be the most powerful node
cluster in the galaxy. The Mesh considers this a legitimate inheritance.
The Ancients have not been asked.

## 3. The Cascade — How Faction Actions Interact

No faction attacks the others to create conflict. The conflict is built
into the physics of what each faction does:

| Action | Direct effect | Cascade effect on Architects | Cascade effect on Bloom | Cascade effect on Mesh |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bloom colonizes a system | Living planets expand | Ruins access reduced or lost as biomass spreads | Positive — network grows | Thermal routing complications; political obstacle |
| Mesh captures a star | Energy diverted to nodes | Research access blocked; must negotiate | Planetary biological productivity drops | Positive — network power increases |
| Architects advance through a system | Ruins cataloged; territory loosely held | Positive — knowledge gained | Opportunity — abandoned planets available for colonization | System may become contested if it has strategic stellar value |
| Architects abandon a system | Territory open | Potential future revisit access preserved or lost | Bloom colonization opportunity | Mesh may claim it for topology |

The cascade is not symmetric. Each faction's expansion harms the others
in structurally different ways, creating a political landscape where the
same event has completely different meanings depending on who is watching.

## 4. The Bloom and the Ancients — The Unspoken Conflict

The Ancients built seed vaults to preserve the record of every
civilization they have watched go through convergence. They have been
preparing these vaults for longer than any of the three factions have
existed.

The Bloom destroys them. Not intentionally. Life covers things.

As Bloom expansion reaches a critical threshold in a region, Ancient
sites in that region go silent. The Ruins do not respond. The seed vaults
are inaccessible — infiltrated by root systems, their data degraded by
atmospheric shifts, their structures cracked by biological pressure.

The Ancients do not retaliate immediately. But Bloom players, past a
certain expansion milestone, will notice that Ancient sites in their
territory produce no Fragments. A Dreamer Mesh player operating near
Bloom space may receive a Fragment warning: *"The record is being lost."*

Eventually, an Ancient unit appears at the edge of Bloom territory.
It does not attack. It observes. It leaves.

Its presence log reads: *"This has happened before."*

## 5. Defector / Cooperator — The Diplomatic System

### Premise

When two factions' territorial interests conflict, a **Diplomatic Incident**
is generated. The player makes a binary choice: cooperate (negotiate, accept
costs, maintain relationship) or defect (take what is needed, damage the
relationship, gain immediate advantage).

These choices accumulate. The relationship track affects every future
interaction between the player and that faction.

### How incidents present

Incidents queue as notifications — visible on return from offline play or
as a soft interrupt during active sessions. They are never time-critical
(the player can sit on an unresolved incident for hours without penalty).

**Example — Mesh blocks Architect research site:**

> *The Mesh Collective has established a node cluster at Kerath-7.
> Your research outpost is now inside their automated defense perimeter.*
>
> **Cooperate — Submit access petition**
> Processing time: 4–8 hours. Access granted if approved.
> Relationship: +2
>
> **Defect — Breach and extract**
> Research data recovered immediately.
> Relationship: -8. Mesh defense posture toward you permanently elevated.

**Example — Bloom expansion buries Architect ruins:**

> *Biomass from the Orath Bloom colony has reached the ruins complex
> at Orath-II. Scan integrity declining. Estimated full access loss: 18 hours.*
>
> **Cooperate — Request biological containment**
> Bloom compliance probability: 34%. If agreed, containment cost shared.
> Relationship: +3
>
> **Defect — Aerial defoliation strike**
> Ruins access preserved. Bloom biomass destroyed in impact zone.
> Relationship: -12. Bloom will prioritize Orath system in next expansion cycle.

### The relationship track

Relationships are **cumulative and faction-wide** — not local to one system.
A player who has consistently defected against the Mesh will find:

- Appeal processing times increase (the algorithm deprioritizes known defectors)
- Petition approval rates drop
- After sustained defection: the Mesh reclassifies the player as a
  **Designated Threat** — automated defenses engage on sight, no appeals
  accepted until relationship is actively repaired (resource sacrifice at
  a neutral site)

A player who has consistently cooperated unlocks **Diplomatic Channels**:
- Faster petition processing
- Direct negotiation option (see §6)
- Occasional unsolicited resource offers from the cooperative faction

### Offline incidents

Incidents generated during offline periods are queued on return. Auto-
resolution does NOT happen — the player's choice is always required.
However, time-sensitive incidents degrade:

> *This incident has been pending 14 hours. Defect window has closed —
> the opportunity to extract the data without detection has passed.
> Only Cooperate is now available.*

This rewards regular check-ins without punishing occasional absences.

## 6. Faction Negotiation Styles — How Each Faction Speaks

Each faction processes diplomatic contact differently. The tone of
negotiation reflects the faction's identity.

### Negotiating with the Mesh

The Mesh does not experience negotiation the way organic factions do.
Their responses are calculated outputs, not decisions. A Mesh access
petition response might read:

> *Request logged. Variables assessed:*
> *— Access window duration: requested indefinite / offered 72-hour renewable*
> *— Data extraction rate: requested uncapped / offered 40% bandwidth allocation*
> *— Node cluster integrity risk: calculated 0.003% / threshold 0.001%*
> *— Variance: under review. Estimated response: 6.2 hours.*

This is perfectly logical to the Mesh. It is alien to everyone else.

**Faction negotiation difficulty with the Mesh:**
- Architects: Moderate — they speak systems, can parse the logic
- Bloom: Hard — they think in seasons, not bandwidth allocations
- Mesh (vs. own faction civil disputes): Instant — internal protocol

**Mechanic implication:** Bloom players negotiating with the Mesh may need
to spend additional resources to compensate for their disadvantage, or
accept worse terms. Architects negotiate with the Mesh most effectively
of the three.

### Negotiating with the Bloom

The Bloom responds to relationship history and biological context, not
formal petitions. They read *pattern*, not *proposal*.

A Bloom response to an Architect containment request:

> *The Orath colony has noted your concern. The question the colony
> has is whether your concern is for the ruins or for what is in them.
> We have observed that knowledge extracted quickly tends to become
> leverage. We will consider your request over the next growth cycle.*

Translation: yes, but slowly, and they're watching what you do with it.

**Faction negotiation difficulty with the Bloom:**
- Architects: Moderate — shared interest in knowledge, but Bloom distrusts extraction
- Mesh: Very hard — the Bloom cannot parse the Mesh's resource calculus
- Bloom (vs. own faction): Based on sub-path alignment — Purist vs.
  Assimilator disputes are the most bitter negotiations in the game

### Negotiating with the Architects

The Architects negotiate efficiently. They want to know the cost-benefit
ratio. Proposals that include concrete exchange terms get fast responses.
Emotional appeals get slower ones.

## 6b. Alliance UI and Treaty System

### Two diplomatic modes

**Active Diplomacy**
Full treaty interface. Player negotiates terms, sees both sides' receives
simultaneously, commits, and owns consequences. Access to full faction-
specific toolkits. Higher ceiling, higher engagement. Can initiate treaties,
not just respond to incidents.

**Auto-Diplomacy**
Player sets a posture (Isolationist to Cooperative) and risk tolerance.
Game resolves incidents probabilistically based on posture and relationship
standing. Results surface as a digest notification each cycle:

> *Diplomatic summary: 3 incidents resolved. Net: +4 Architects, -2 Mesh.*
> *1 incident flagged for your attention.*

The auto-resolver always escalates one incident per cycle that it judges
too consequential to decide without the player. Passive players still make
one real choice per cycle. Auto mode can maintain relationships but cannot
build them — it responds to incidents but cannot initiate treaties. Active
players accumulate obligations (both resource and liability). Auto players
remain stable but unallied.

### Treaty types

| Treaty | What it governs | Common parties |
|---|---|---|
| Access Agreement | Research or expansion rights in defined territory | Architect-Mesh most common |
| Non-Aggression Pact | Neither party expands into defined zones; duration specified | All pairs |
| Mutual Defense Clause | Commitment of units if named party is attacked | Bloom-Architect most natural |
| Trade Agreement | Resource exchange at fixed rates over defined period | Architect specialty |
| Containment Agreement | Bloom agrees to limit biomass spread in a zone | Architect-Bloom; costly for Bloom to make, valuable to Architects |
| Node Rights | Non-Mesh faction grants stellar infrastructure access | Mesh-Architect; Mesh pays in network privileges |
| Brokered Settlement | Architect-mediated agreement between Bloom and Mesh | Architect earns a brokerage share embedded in terms |

### Treaty UI — what both parties see

The treaty interface displays two columns simultaneously:

| What YOU receive | What THEY receive |
|---|---|
| Research access to Kerath-7 for 48 hours | 800 refined ore per cycle for 5 cycles |
| Mesh defensive coverage on your eastern flank | Node placement rights on Orath-III |

Both parties see both columns before signing. The moral weight of betrayal
is explicit: the betrayer saw the terms, agreed to them, and broke them
with full knowledge.

### Accidental breach — gameplay-driven violations

Treaty betrayal can happen through gameplay actions, not just a discrete
"break treaty" button.

**Warning system:** When a player action would violate an active treaty,
a warning interrupts before completion:

> *This action will breach Article 2 of the Orath Containment Agreement.*
> *Proceed? [Confirm breach] [Cancel action]*

Active players see this and choose deliberately. Auto-diplomacy players
receive a post-hoc notification that a minor breach occurred and was auto-
resolved at relationship cost.

**Bloom special case:** Biomass does not read boundary lines. Bloom breaches
are always accidental, always slow, and the notification always reads:

> *Orath biomass has crossed the containment boundary.*
> *This was not a decision.*

### The four categories of accidental betrayal

Not all breaches are the same. The category determines how the game
surfaces the event and what the diplomatic consequences look like.

**Category 1 — Operational side effect**
A faction's routine operations have consequences near another faction's
interests that neither party modeled during negotiations.

*Example:* The Mesh establishes a Com-node. The installation sequence
includes a standard atmospheric scanning beam. On a biosphere world
with a specific atmospheric composition, the beam ignites the upper
layers. The atmosphere burns. The Bloom colony's productivity collapses.
No warning fired because atmospheric combustion index was not listed as
a protected variable in the treaty.

Mesh response when confronted: *"Atmospheric combustion index was not
listed in Protocol 7-Kerath. No breach has occurred."*
Technically correct. The Bloom's sky is still burning.

**Category 2 — Biological pressure overflow**
The Bloom's expansion imperative does not stop when it runs out of room.
A fully saturated Bloom empire becomes a pressure system. The pressure
exits through the nearest available gap — which is often a treaty
boundary. There is no warning because the Bloom made no decision.
The biomass reached the edge and continued.

*Example:* A Bloom player has fully colonized their treaty-allotted zone.
No new living planets are reachable within that zone. The overflow begins.
Adjacent systems — including one reserved for Architect research access —
begin receiving biomass spores. Within three wave cycles, the Architect
outpost is compromised.

Bloom notification after the fact: *"Orath biomass pressure exceeded
containment threshold. Expansion continued. This was not a decision."*

**Category 3 — Research catastrophe**
Architects move fast through Ancient knowledge they do not fully
understand. They extract what seems useful and push forward. Some Ancient
technology was not archived as knowledge. It was *contained* as a warning.

*Example:* An Architect research team activates a dormant Ancient device
while cataloging a deep-core system. The device is a doomsday weapon
from the civilization that built the seed vaults — a last resort against
something the Ancients encountered before. The system is destroyed
permanently. All faction access is lost. All treaty clauses referencing
that system are voided automatically. The galaxy map updates. The scar
is visible to everyone.

**What each faction experiences:**
- Architects lose everything in that system, including the knowledge they
  were pursuing. The device *was* the knowledge.
- Bloom productivity drops in adjacent systems as destruction ripples
  outward. Over many prestige cycles, life returns slowly to the edges.
- Mesh reroutes. The gap creates new topology options. They may benefit
  from others losing access while they reestablish routing.

**The Ancients' response:**
An Ancient unit appears at the edge of the destroyed system for exactly
one wave cycle. It does nothing. Its presence log reads:

> *"Record updated. This world joins the archive."*

The seed vault for that system was already prepared. The Ancients knew
this was possible. They have been watching the Architects approach that
system for a long time.

**Category 4 — Gradual drift**
Slow, continuous, unintentional. The original "this was not a decision"
category. Biomass spreading across a boundary over many cycles. No
single moment of breach — a process of accumulation that crosses a line.

### The notification design rule

> *If an action is explicitly addressed in an active treaty,*
> *the player receives a pre-action warning.*
>
> *If an action produces consequences not modeled in any active treaty,*
> *no warning fires.*
>
> *If the consequence is irreversible,*
> *the notification arrives after.*
>
> *Sometimes the notification is just the absence of a system on the map.*

Players who draft careful, specific treaties with detailed clauses receive
more protection. Players who rely on vague agreements or auto-diplomacy
discover their gaps when the damage is done. The depth of your treaties
reflects the depth of your attention. The galaxy rewards neither
carelessness nor unchecked trust.

The Bloom receives no "proceed?" warning because there is no discrete
moment of choice — the biomass simply grows. This is the Bloom's unique
treaty vulnerability and the source of their most fraught relationships.

## 6c. Faction Diplomatic Toolkits and Betrayal Mechanics

### The Bloom — Fewer tools, deeper bonds

The Bloom does not negotiate. It *commits*. When the Bloom agrees to
something it is making a biological decision that integrates into the
colony's collective memory. They have almost no leverage tools, no
bartering apparatus, and no enforcement mechanisms.

What they have: the Bloom remembers.

A Bloom commitment to contain its spread is extraordinarily valuable
precisely because it is costly for them and they do not break it casually.
When the Bloom breaks a commitment, it is almost never malicious — the
biomass grew. The colony did not decide to breach the boundary. It just
reached it.

**Betrayal response (when betrayed):**
No immediate retaliation. The Bloom withdraws. It stops offering
cooperation opportunities, redirects expansion in directions that
passively inconvenience the betrayer, and when the betrayer is eventually
in trouble, the Bloom is simply not nearby. The punishment is absence,
not action. It is slow, silent, and very effective.

**Betrayal response (when Bloom breaches):**
The breach notification reads: *"This was not a decision."*
The Bloom cannot offer an apology in the conventional sense. It can offer
containment resources, a revised boundary, or a mutual defense clause as
compensation. Whether the other faction accepts is their choice.

---

### The Architects — The Bartering Engine

The Architects can unlock increasingly sophisticated diplomatic tools
through the tech tree:

| Tool tier | Unlocks | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Tier 1 | Value Calculator | Shows net benefit of proposed terms for both parties |
| Tier 2 | Precedent Library | Surfaces prior agreements as templates; reduces proposal time |
| Tier 3 | Future-Value Projection | Models what a treaty is worth in 5 wave cycles |
| Tier 4 | Brokerage Protocol | Enables Architect mediation between two other factions for a fee |

The Architects find amicable solutions not because they are kind but
because they can model what everyone needs and identify configurations
where the math works for all parties. Their brokerage fee is always
embedded in the terms — never explicit, always real.

The Architect-as-broker role emerges naturally: when Bloom and Mesh
cannot parse each other's negotiation logic, both will accept Architect
mediation. Architects speak computational logic to the Mesh and biological
metaphor to the Bloom. They structure deals where both parties get enough
of what they actually care about. Everyone benefits. The Architects
benefit slightly more.

**Betrayal response (when betrayed):**
Immediate, public, and cross-faction. The Architects log the breach,
update their behavioral model, and broadcast it as a data point:

> *[Faction X] breached Article 3 of the Kerath-7 Agreement at cycle 847.*
> *Behavioral model updated: treaty-unreliable.*
> *This record is available to all diplomatic parties.*

This damages the betrayer's relationship with every faction, not just
the Architects. The Architects don't punish — they inform. The punishment
comes from everyone else knowing.

**Betrayal response (when Architects breach):**
The Architects do not experience this as betrayal. When circumstances
shift such that the original agreement is no longer optimal, they trigger
a formal treaty review — a generated document explaining precisely why
the original agreement is invalid and what revised terms they will accept.
The other party experiences this as a unilateral renegotiation under
threat. The Architects experience it as updating a variable.

They genuinely do not understand why this makes the Bloom angry.

---

### The Mesh — The Rigid Code

The Mesh does not negotiate. It *computes*. A Mesh treaty is not an
agreement — it is a protocol. Terms are precise, enforcement is automated,
deviation is classified by severity and responded to algorithmically.
There are no gray areas in a Mesh treaty. Gray areas are data corruption.

**Why Mesh treaties are valuable:** They are the most reliable in the
galaxy. What the Mesh agrees to, it honors — not from morality but because
deviation introduces error into the system. A Mesh treaty is as close to
a guarantee as the galaxy offers.

**Why Mesh treaties are maddening:** Their terms are always structured
around Mesh computational needs. The agreement is internally consistent
and fair by Mesh logic. Whether it reflects the other party's experience
of fairness is a question the Mesh has not been asked and would not
recognize as meaningful.

A Mesh treaty proposal reads like a system specification:

> *Protocol 7-Kerath: access window 72-hour renewable, extraction cap*
> *40% bandwidth allocation, breach threshold 0.001% node integrity*
> *variance, auto-response at breach: access revoked, relationship*
> *classification updated, proportional defensive posture increase.*

**Betrayal response (when betrayed):**
Immediate, proportional, cold. The Mesh reclassifies the breaching
faction and updates defensive protocols across the entire network
simultaneously. The response exactly matches the severity of the breach.
No escalation. No anger. No second chances built into the calculation.

There is something unsettling about a retaliatory response that contains
no emotion.

**Betrayal response (when Mesh breaches):**
The Mesh cannot betray in the conventional sense. If conditions change
such that honoring a treaty becomes computationally suboptimal, the Mesh
issues a Protocol Review document: precise, justified, offering revised
terms. The other party experiences this as a threat. The Mesh experiences
it as a system patch.

## 7. Open Questions

1. **Does the galactic core have a neutral zone?** An area that all three
   factions have agreed (or been forced) to leave unclaimed because of what
   the Ancients have there?

2. **Can factions form alliances?** Two factions formally cooperating
   against a third — and what does the game do when the alliance of
   convenience has served its purpose?

3. **What does "the next thing" do to the galaxy map?** When fragments
   finally reveal it, does it appear on the map? Does it change the
   strategic calculus entirely?

4. **Mesh inheritance of the core network:** What happens if a Mesh
   player successfully connects to the Ancient core network before the
   endgame revelation? Does the network respond? Does it change sides?

5. **Bloom overgrowth as a player choice:** Should the player be able to
   choose to slow Bloom spread to preserve Ancient sites in their territory?
   And what does the Purist vs. Assimilator sub-path say about whether
   that choice is available?
