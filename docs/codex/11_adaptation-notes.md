# 11 — Adaptation Notes

> The Codex's only section that touches mechanics. Sections 1–10 are
> pure canon — true in any medium. This section maps that canon onto
> four specific media: the **game** (the realized adaptation), a
> **tabletop / game-master** use, a **graphic novel**, and **young
> adult fiction.** Each is given equal treatment. None is privileged;
> the canon is the spine, and each medium is one body it can wear.
>
> The recurring problem of adapting this IP is always the same: the
> reveal must be *earned, never told* (Section 8). Each medium solves
> that problem with different tools. The four sub-sections below are
> mostly about those tools.

---

## 11.1 — The Game (the realized adaptation)

The game is the adaptation the design corpus (`core/10`–`core/22`)
fully specifies. It is documented here so an adapter in another medium
can see *one* complete solution to the earned-reveal problem before
attempting their own.

**The form.** An idle-miner / tower-defense / endless-wave hybrid,
built for a player with limited session time. Three simultaneous
layers — idle production that runs even while away, passive tower
defense, and active unit production on cooldown — so that a five-
minute session and a one-hour session are both "playing the game."

**How the canon becomes mechanics.**

- **The cadet and the Academy** become the opening: a faction-less
  player is sorted by *behavior* in short scenario tests, then commits
  to a faction and an internal path.
- **The Fractured Age** becomes the moment-to-moment game: building,
  surviving escalating waves, navigating treaties and the cascade.
- **The wave commanders** become the enemy: named figures, one
  appearance each, two spoken lines apiece, accumulating into the
  "opera of voices" that is the enemy civilization.
- **The endless structure** becomes *prestige*: a run ends in a
  **Collapse**, and the player begins again. There is no win state —
  correct for an endless game and correct for a cosmology with no
  victory, only a threshold.
- **The reveal** is delivered across prestiges. One **Fragment** per
  Collapse, seven total. **Memory Tiers** model the player's
  deepening interpretation — old Fragments gain new annotations as
  the player goes deeper, so the same artifact re-read means more.
- **The Mark** is lit, circle by circle, by the player having played
  *all three factions* — the recognition gated behind literally
  having been the others.
- **The Ancients** are a reactive system: the **Dominance Meter** and
  **pacification** rituals at the Ruins keep them present without
  making them an enemy.

**The crucial discipline.** The game uses **no cutscenes.** The story
lives in the seams of play — commander lines, Fragment drops, the
desaturation when an Ancient appears, the floor of the Pilgrimage.
This is the game's answer to "earned, never told," and it is the
single most important thing another medium should *learn from* even
though no other medium can copy it directly.

## 11.2 — Tabletop / Game-Master Use

The canon is built to be system-neutral, which makes it strong
tabletop material. It can support a faction-political campaign, an
exploration campaign through the Ruins, a single cadet's coming-of-age
arc, or a slow-burn cosmic-dread campaign — on any ruleset.

**The setting as a sandbox.** The galaxy (Section 4) is the campaign
map: a gradient pulling everyone toward a dangerous center, three
factions whose every act of self-interest harms the others, a
forbidden Core. A GM does not need a plot — the **cascade** generates
conflict on its own. Drop a player group into the Fractured Age and
the structural friction does the work.

**The convergence as a doom clock.** The single best mechanical hook
the canon offers a GM is the **Approach** (Section 7) as a campaign
clock. The silence advances; the aperture has shifted; the threshold
is coming. Most systems already have a clock/doom-track mechanic —
the silence vector slots straight into it. The party's choices speed
or slow nothing about the Arrival itself; what they affect is whether
the civilization is *converging or fracturing further* as it runs out.

**The Ancients as a GM instrument.** The Ancients are the GM's most
useful tool: a presence that can appear, deliver one recontextualizing
line, and leave. Use them exactly as the canon does — rarely, briefly,
never as quest-givers. An Ancient observer at the table is a way to
turn the lights down for a sentence.

**The Fragments as physical handouts.** The seven Fragments (Section
8) are tailor-made as **props**: hand the players a printed log, an
instrument readout, an image, a sample description. Pace them across
sessions, one every few sessions, exactly as the game paces them
across prestiges. The Mark works as a handout too — give the players
a drawing of three overlapping circles early, with no explanation,
and let it sit on the table for a campaign.

**The GM's discipline.** The earned-reveal rule becomes a GM rule:
**never narrate the conclusion.** Describe the wrong-sized doorways;
do not say "the builders were not human." Show the heretic faction;
do not explain the seam. The GM's hardest and most important job in
this setting is to hold the line on the unsaid (Section 9). If the
table arrives at "we were one people" on its own, the campaign has
succeeded. If the GM says it, it has not.

**A note on player factions.** Letting each player be a different
faction is the tabletop version of multi-faction play — the party
*is* the three substrates, and the campaign's emotional arc is the
party discovering it was always one thing. This is the strongest
single structural choice a GM can make with this canon.

## 11.3 — Graphic Novel

The IP is unusually strong visual-narrative material, because so much
of its canon is *already image*: the Mark, the desaturation, the
wrong-scaled architecture, the silence.

**The visual language is pre-built.** Section 4 hands an artist a
complete palette: the low-albedo stone that *absorbs* light and casts
shadow inward; structures 15–20% too large; seamless joins with no
tool marks; the celestial aperture. The three factions have fixed
visual identities — Architect cold precision and amber, Bloom forest
green and bioluminescence, Mesh near-black and electric blue. An
artist should treat these as canon and the **three substrates of the
Mark** as the key art of the entire property.

**Staging the reveal in panels.** The graphic novel's superpower is
the *recontextualizing image* — a panel that means one thing on first
read and another on a re-read. Plant the Mark in an early establishing
shot, uncaptioned, small. Plant the three substrates separately, in
three faction-introduction sequences, before ever showing them
together. The page where the audience connects them should carry no
narration at all. The medium's version of "earned, never told" is
**the wordless panel that the reader completes.**

**Voice as lettering.** The faction voices (Section 5) become
lettering and balloon design: the Architects' even, engineered type;
the Bloom's organic, irregular forms; the Mesh's fragmented,
frame-late, glitched lettering. The late commander "cracks" can be
shown typographically — a Mesh balloon resolving *too* cleanly, a
Bloom balloon with a word in it that belongs to no faction.

**Silence as white space.** The Arrival must never be drawn (Section
10). The graphic novel renders it as *absence*: empty panels, a page
that goes white, a system shown twice — once full of light, once
identical and empty. The instruments still recording nothing become a
panel of a readout at zero, held, and held, and held.

**The closing image.** The canon already specifies the perfect final
page: the Pilgrimage floor, the three-circle Mark, and the celestial
aperture's light slowly moving across it. End there. No caption.

**Pacing.** The seven Fragments map naturally onto seven chapters or
issues; the Address is the climax of the penultimate one; the
postscript belongs to the final page of the series.

## 11.4 — Young Adult Fiction

The IP's emotional architecture is, almost suspiciously, a YA novel
already. The Academy cadet origin is a coming-of-age frame; the
faction sort is the oldest YA structure there is; and the central
theme — *what kind of person are you, when no one has told you?* — is
the genre's defining question.

**The protagonist.** The cadet (Section 6) becomes a first-person YA
narrator: faction-less, observed, uncertain, sorted. The canon keeps
the cadet deliberately blank; YA fills the blank with a specific,
voiced teenager, and that is not a violation — the cadet's *specifics*
are explicitly flexible (Section 10). The only fixed thing is the
trajectory: faction-less, then sorted, then — across the arc —
exposed to all three.

**The faction sort as the inciting incident.** Being sorted by
behavior rather than by choice is a rich, slightly unsettling YA
premise: an institution watched you and decided who you are. The
canon detail that the Academy is *funded by all three factions and
each interprets graduates differently* gives the novel its first
conflict — the protagonist never being sure what made them, or for
whom.

**Multi-faction play, in prose.** The novel cannot have the reader
literally replay as three factions. It translates the structure into
**relationships and movement**: a protagonist with a foot in more
than one faction — friends, family, mentors, a transfer, a defection,
a love interest across the line. The estranged-family theme (Section
9) is the engine. The reader should come to recognize the kinship
through *people*, by caring about characters on three sides of a war.

**The reveal across a trilogy.** Three books map cleanly: Book One,
total immersion in one faction and the protagonist's sorting (Option
A is the whole world). Book Two, the cracks — the heresies, a
friendship that should not make sense, the first Fragments. Book
Three, the Address and the recognition. The forbidden sentence —
*you have been fighting yourself* — is the one line the trilogy is
built to make the reader think and the prose must never print.

**Tone for the age band.** Keep the canon's melancholy but lead with
its hope. YA can hold real dread — the silence, the emptied worlds —
but the genre wants the protagonist's agency and the possibility of
the better outcome foregrounded. The Ancients' hypothesis, untested
but real, is the novel's source of hope. Hold it exactly where the
canon holds it: believed, unconfirmed, worth everything.

## A Cross-Medium Closing Note

Whatever the medium, four things travel with the IP and must arrive
intact:

1. **The audience must be allowed to believe Option A first** — fully,
   for a long time.
2. **The reveal must be assembled by the audience, never delivered to
   them.** Every medium has its own version of the wordless panel.
3. **The Arrival must never be shown.**
4. **The last beat is the question, not the answer.**

An adaptation that keeps those four has kept the IP, whatever else it
changes. An adaptation that loses any one of them has built something
else — possibly something good, but not this.
