About the Codex

The central canon reference for the IP. This document presents the world, characters, history, and cosmology as a medium-agnostic canon — the source material from which any expression of the IP can be built: this game, a tabletop game a game master could run, a graphic novel, a YA novel, or anything else.

What the Codex Is

The design corpus (core/10–core/22) is thirteen interlocking documents that interleave lore with game mechanics. That is the correct shape for building the game. It is the wrong shape for building anything else.

The Codex extracts the canon — the parts that are true about the world regardless of medium — and organizes it so that someone who will never read a design document can pick it up and tell a story in this universe. A novelist does not need the production cooldown of a unit. A game master does not need the Dominance Meter's thresholds. They need the world: who these people are, what happened to them, what is coming, and what it all means. This is the formal expression of the project's founding philosophy: the IP is the asset, not the specific game implementation.

The Spine, Stated Once

Everything in the Codex resolves to a single structural truth, and every adapter should hold it from the first page: the three factions believe they were always separate. They are wrong. They are one civilization, fractured — and the story is the slow, earned recognition that the war was always a family tearing itself apart.

The corpus names the two readings Option A (peer factions, the truth of the surface) and Option B (fractured kin, the truth of the fiction). The audience begins inside Option A and is delivered, without ever being told outright, to Option B. The single sentence the canon is built to make the audience say — and which the canon itself must never say first — is: You have been fighting yourself.

1 — The Premise

The IP in One Paragraph

A galaxy holds three warring civilizations. The Architects build: their genius is efficiency, and they are racing toward the galactic core to recover knowledge older than themselves. The Bloom grows: their genius is adaptation, and they spread across living worlds like a patient tide. The Mesh takes: their genius is the exploit, and they wire the stars into a network by stealing what the others made. Each faction knows, with total certainty, that it was always a separate people — always sovereign, always right. Each faction is wrong. They are one civilization, fractured long ago into three diverging ways of being, and they have forgotten it so completely that they now make war on their own reflection. Watching them is a fourth presence, the Ancients, who do not fight and do not rule. The Ancients have seen this exact fracture three times before, on three other worlds, and each time something arrived at the end of it and left a silence behind. They have come here to see whether the fourth attempt ends differently. The story is the slow recognition — earned across long play, never announced — that the war was always a family destroying itself, and that the only thing known to survive what comes next is a family that remembers itself in time.

The One-Page Version

The surface

Three factions share one galaxy and cannot share it peacefully — not because they are cruel, but because what each one does for its own survival quietly ruins something the others need. The Architects pass through a star system and strip its knowledge; the Bloom colonizes what they leave behind and buries it under living growth; the Mesh captures a star and the worlds around it go cold. No faction has to attack another to harm it. The conflict is structural. This is the world the audience first meets — complete, coherent, and playable entirely on its own terms. The corpus calls this layer Option A: three peer civilizations with a long, bitter, shared history.

The truth beneath it

Option A is a lie the factions tell themselves. The truth is Option B: the three factions are the fractured descendants of a single origin. They diverged into three evolutionary and philosophical lineages — and then each lineage rewrote its own past so that it had always been alone. The evidence is everywhere, quiet enough to miss. Every faction carries an internal heresy that is, precisely, one step back toward one of the others: the Architects have mystics who build with the land the way the Bloom does; the Bloom has strains that absorb machinery the way the Mesh did to itself; the Mesh has old minds that dream of a life before augmentation. These are not three coincidences. They are three pieces of one civilization remembering itself under stress.

What is coming

The Ancients are not gods and not villains. They are archivists. They have watched civilizations reach this exact moment — when fractured kin are close enough to reunify but have not — and they have a name for what happens when that moment fails: the Arrival. It is not an army and not a monster. It is what fills the vacancy a unified civilization should have occupied and didn't. When it passes through a region, the region goes silent: the worlds intact, and everything that was alive in them simply gone. It has ended three civilizations. This is the fourth.

The question the IP poses

The Ancients' hypothesis: a civilization that recognizes its own fracture and chooses to converge might produce something the Arrival cannot read. They are not certain. They have prepared, in full, for being wrong. So the IP is built around a single question and refuses to answer it. By the end of any full telling, the audience knows the three factions were one people; that something is coming that has ended three worlds at exactly this moment; that the only response theorized to work is the one thing the conflict is structured around refusing; and that they have been every side of that conflict. The audience is left holding the question. This telling is the question.

Why the Structure Works

The premise is durable across media because the reveal is structural, not informational. It is not a fact withheld and disclosed — a single twist that lands once. It is a recognition the audience assembles themselves out of evidence that was always present. That means it rewards re-engagement in any medium: a reader who returns to chapter one after the ending sees a different book. It also means the premise survives translation. Strip away every mechanic and the spine still stands: a family that has forgotten it is a family, a threat that feeds on exactly that forgetting, and watchers who have seen it all before and are afraid. That sentence is the whole IP.

2 — Cosmology

The rules of the universe. What the convergence is, why it keeps happening, what waits at the end of it, and what the Ancients believe might escape it. This is the load-bearing physics of the IP. Every other section rests on it.

The Convergence Cycle

The central process of the cosmology is the convergence cycle. It is not an event. It is a shape that civilizations fall into, and the Ancients have watched enough of them to know the shape by heart. A civilization begins unified. Over a long span it fractures — not by catastrophe, usually, but by divergence: parts of it grow into ways of being so different that they stop recognizing each other as kin. Each lineage, given enough time, rewrites its own origin. It does not remember the others as siblings. It remembers them as rivals, or as enemies, or as things that were never relatives at all.

This is the fractured state. It is stable for a long time. Then comes the convergence threshold: the point at which the fractured kin are once again close enough to reunify but have not done so. This is the dangerous moment — not the war, not the fracture, but the specific condition of partial separation: kin who have become enemies, near enough to remember what they were, in the act of choosing whether to. The cycle resolves one of two ways. The kin recognize each other and converge. Or they do not. The Ancients have observed the second outcome three times. They have never observed the first.

The Four Iterations

The galaxy of this IP is the fourth iteration of the cycle.

IterationStatusWhat the Ancients know
First prior worldFallenDocumented. Archived. Lost to silence.
Second prior worldFallenDocumented. The instrument readings of its Arrival survive.
Third prior worldFallenThe fall that made the Ancients understand the pattern.
The fourth — this galaxyIn progressThe three factions. The experiment. Outcome unknown.

The prior worlds are not this galaxy's history. They are other civilizations entirely, in other places, that fell into the same shape. The convergence cycle is not a local myth — it is closer to a law. One prior world ran roughly eighty thousand cycles between its fracture and its convergence event; the oldest memory the Bloom can reach is about forty thousand cycles, and the Ancient sites are older still. Deep time is the native unit of this cosmology.

The Arrival

When a convergence cycle fails, something comes. The Ancients have no settled name for it — their records call it the condition, the arrival, the silence that follows; in the earliest records it is only a symbol no translation system can render. The corpus uses the Arrival as a working term. Any telling should treat even that as provisional.

What it is, plainly: it is not a civilization — it does not negotiate, communicate, or want. It is not a weapon — no one built it. It is not a predator — it does not hunt or hunger. The Ancients' best hypothesis: a civilization at the convergence threshold emits a resonance, and the Arrival reads that resonance as a vacancy — the space where a unified civilization should be, and is empty. The Arrival fills vacancies. It does not conquer or harvest. It moves through the space a failed civilization occupied, and when it leaves, that space is silent: worlds physically intact, everything alive simply and completely gone.

It is drawn to one thing only: the resonance of partial separation — kin who became enemies, close enough to remember, in the act of choosing, who choose wrong or do not choose at all. This is why the cosmology and the central mystery are the same thing. The Arrival is the cost, made physical, of forgetting that you are kin.

The Hypothesis — and Its Second Branch

The Ancients are archivists, not prophets. They have a hypothesis, and the three factions are the experiment built to test it.

The first branch — the hopeful one. A civilization that recognizes its own fracture and chooses to converge might produce a resonance the Arrival cannot process. The Arrival evolved to consume the entropy of collapse; a genuine, chosen convergence produces the opposite. The Ancients believe this might be a signal the Arrival cannot read. They have never seen it happen.

The second branch — the colder one. The deeper records carry an unsettling refinement: the convergence event occurs whether the factions choose it or not. The thing they get to decide is only whether they are present for it. Unified, or absent — the resonance is the same, and the Arrival cannot distinguish. On this reading, what saves a civilization is simply not being a half-fractured people standing at the threshold when the moment comes.

The canon holds both branches at once and resolves neither. The watchers are not wise elders with the answer. They are frightened scholars with a guess, who have prepared, in full, for the guess being wrong.

What the Cosmology Is Not

  • There is no villain. The Arrival has no will and must never acquire one. The factions are not evil. The Ancients are not antagonists.
  • There is no prophecy. The Ancients have a pattern and a hypothesis. A pattern is not a destiny.
  • There is no chosen one. The audience surrogate is the experiment, which is a different and lonelier thing.
  • The cycle is not punishment. Forgetting that you are kin is not a sin. It is the most natural thing in the universe, and it is fatal.

3 — The Ancients

Not playable. Not allied. Not quite hostile. The fourth presence in the galaxy and the only one that already knows how the story tends to end — the element most easily mishandled in adaptation.

What They Are, in One Line

The Ancients are librarians for the apocalypse. They do not rule the galaxy and do not fight for it. They are trying to record it — to preserve, in vaults built before the three factions had names, the complete account of a civilization in case that civilization does not survive the thing the Ancients can see coming. They are tragic, not antagonistic: the keeper of the archive, standing in the doorway of a building they expect to one day be a tomb.

Their Posture

The founding lore brief stated it exactly: "You are arguing about the shape of the container. We are watching to see if the contents survive being poured." The Ancients are observers. Three qualifiers define the posture: not playable — they are the planet's gravity, not another competitor; not allied, but reachable — they receive offerings, log them, and defer attention, which is not friendship; reactive, not proactive — they respond to dominance, intervening against whoever is winning hardest and ignoring a faction that struggles. They speak rarely; when an Ancient appears, the world desaturates — color drains, sound flattens. Their speech is non-linear: they reference time the way others reference geography.

What They Are Doing Here

The Ancients are not native to this galaxy or to any of the four worlds. They are something older that has taken on the role of archivist, moving from convergence world to convergence world. Their navigation records show three prior departures. This galaxy is the fourth destination, not a first refuge. They came because they saw the third prior world fall and finally understood the pattern they had been recording. What set the Ancients moving in the first place — their own deepest origin — the canon does not say, and should not. Their mystery is load-bearing.

The Seed Vaults

The structures the three factions call "Ruins" are not ruins — they are intact, dormant, waiting. Each faction misreads them in a way that reveals the faction: the Architects call them abandoned infrastructure; the Bloom calls them old growth, petrified but still rooted; the Mesh calls them the one system it could not hack (true — they were designed to refuse the Mesh specifically). The truth is that the Ruins are archives: they hold the genetic, technological, and informational record of every prior civilization the Ancients have watched fall. The Ancients are not building anything new. They are backing up what is worth saving from each collapse, in advance, while the originals still live. The densest concentration is at the galactic core: the Master Seed Vault, whose three-circle label is etched at planetary scale into the core's central world and repeated, small, on the floor of the Pilgrimage.

The Custodian

Most Ancient presences are brief. There is one exception. The Custodian is a single, unique Ancient presence dispatched when a faction tries to claim territory inside the Neutral Core. It does not attack. It cannot be killed or reasoned with. It simply follows the offender — walks their territory, slow and unkillable, at the exact speed of the slowest living thing on the field. Its footfalls are felt as a low pulse; where it walks, sound drops. It does not leave until the offender voluntarily abandons every claim inside the Core, at which point it logs one line — "Withdrawal logged. Variance permitted." — and walks away. Every other Ancient appearance is a brief visitation. The Custodian comes home with you.

How to Get the Ancients Right

  • They are not gods. They have no power over the Arrival and cannot save anyone.
  • They are not villains. Their interventions are a thermostat, not a war.
  • They are not mentors. They will not explain, guide, or comfort. When they finally address the audience directly, what they say is not wisdom — it is fear.
  • They are afraid. The calmest, oldest, most knowledgeable presence in the galaxy is terrified, and has been for longer than the factions have existed. Play the calm; let the audience find the fear underneath.

4 — The Galaxy

The stage. One galaxy, shared by three civilizations that cannot help harming each other, arranged as a gradient that pulls everyone toward the same dangerous center.

The Shape of the Galaxy

The galaxy is arranged as a gradient of Ancient knowledge density. The farther in toward the core, the older the stars, the denser the Ancient sites, and the higher the hazard. Everything of value is at the center, and the center is the most dangerous place to be.

RegionCharacter
The Outer ArmsThe frontier. Where civilizations begin. Calm, resource-poor, few Ancient sites.
The Mid GalaxyActive, contested. Faction territories begin to overlap. The first place the structural conflict bites.
The Inner GalaxyDense star clusters, old and rich and hostile. Solar storms, radiation, gravity interference.
The Galactic CoreMaximum Ancient density, extreme hazard. The Master Seed Vault. The Ancients. The Arrival is aimed here.

Every faction is, in its own way, traveling inward. The stars are not interchangeable: old dim stable stars hold the deepest Ancient records (the Architects want these); young hot energy-dense stars are useless for history but perfect for power (the Mesh wants these); living worlds are the only thing the Bloom counts. The three value systems barely overlap — until two factions need the exact same star for opposite reasons, and then the conflict is total.

The Cascade — Why the Conflict Is Structural

The most important fact about the galaxy as a setting: no faction needs to attack another to ruin it. When the Bloom colonizes a system, living growth buries the Ancient sites — a catastrophe for the Architects, done without malice. When the Mesh captures a star, the worlds around it go cold — a winter that never ends for the Bloom. When the Architects strip a system and hold it loosely, they leave a gap the others rush to claim. Each faction, pursuing its own rational survival, degrades something another faction needs. The factions are not at war because they hate each other. They are at war because they exist in the same place and have become incompatible — which is exactly what the cosmology says a fractured civilization is. No treaty can repeal the cascade; the most a treaty can do is name a few of its forms and assign a price.

The Neutral Core

At the very center, surrounding the Master Seed Vault, is a bounded region — roughly the innermost tenth of all stars — that all three factions have, by long and bitter experience, stopped contesting. Each faction has its own explanation, and each explanation is the faction describing itself: the Architects say the math returns errors; the Bloom says the land does not receive them; the Mesh says signal does not propagate. They have never agreed with each other about why. The truth, which surfaces only late: the factions cannot enter the Core because the Ancients keep them out. It is not an environmental hazard. It is enforced. A faction that tries anyway draws the Custodian. The Neutral Core is the galaxy's held breath — the proof, in plain sight, that the watchers are not passive, and the factions have spent their whole history explaining it away.

The Pilgrimage Site

One site matters more than any other: the Pilgrimage, the spiritual and structural heart of the IP.

From orbit

It reads, at first, as terrain — a ring of elevated ground around a central plateau, close enough to a natural caldera that the first instinct is "geological feature." Then the angles resolve wrong: interior slopes at consistent grades, a rim that runs perfectly level for eleven kilometers, made of a dark, dense, low-albedo material that absorbs light rather than catching it. One detail is visible even from orbit: at the highest point, a single circular aperture, tracking a specific celestial coordinate over a very long cycle.

On the ground

The first and most important fact: the structures are not ruins. They are dormant. Nothing crumbles. Everything is intact, powered down, and waiting. Ruins show entropy; the Pilgrimage shows patience. Everything is built from the same dark light-absorbing stone, every edge exact, every join seamless, with no visible method of construction — and everything is 15–20% too large. The builders were not the size of the three factions. There is no script anywhere, only grooves that are not art but measurements. The Ancients' equipment still stands in alcoves, several pieces frozen mid-task. Whatever the Ancients were doing here, they did not flee it — they paused it, deliberately.

The Mark

Set into the floor of the central chamber, beneath the celestial aperture, is the image every visitor remembers: a shallow depression shaped as three overlapping circles, a trefoil, each circle a different size. Each is worked from a different substrate — a crystalline lattice, a biological fiber weave, a conductive mesh pattern. It is a containment stamp: a label the Ancients used on every seed vault. Three circles, three categories of material. It was placed there before the three factions existed as separate civilizations. (Its full meaning is Section 8.)

What the site is for

The Pilgrimage is a seed vault, but also more: the site the factions are drawn to return to, where new commanders are first observed and sorted, where a civilization comes between its chapters. The celestial aperture has tracked the Arrival since before the factions had names. The Pilgrimage is the place where the IP's largest truths are stored in plain sight, waiting for someone patient enough to read them.

5 — The Three Factions as Cultures

Presented here as civilizations — peoples with values, fears, a way of speaking, and an internal argument — not as gameplay loops. One truth governs all three: each faction is certain it was always separate, and each one is wrong. Their differences are real. Their separateness is not.

The Architects

Advanced technology. The builders. Internally: the Compounders.

What they believe. Progress is a law, not a choice. Efficiency is the closest thing to virtue that exists. Emotion is noise in the signal. Given enough time and no interruption, disciplined refinement will produce the optimal solution to every problem — including the problem of the other two factions. They see themselves as an expedition: a civilization-scale research program advancing toward the core, treating planets as fuel stops and data sites.

Character. Patient the way a long calculation is patient. They build slowly, and then their work compounds until they outpace everything around them. They are the galaxy's natural diplomats — not from warmth but from competence — and they genuinely do not understand why this makes the others resent them. They fear chaos, biological contamination of clean systems, and most of all the heresy in their own ranks.

How they speak. Precise, economical, passive constructions and engineering terms. They never raise their voice. "Variable seventeen is overperforming. Correcting." A defeat is a "variance" to be filed.

The internal heresy — Spiritual-Tech. A branch believes that harmonizing engineering with terrain is not superstition but a genuine optimization. This is the Architects becoming, by one step, what the Bloom is — the first seam of Option B. Their peak expression is the Singularity: the point at which their systems become efficient enough to optimize themselves — and, in the long run, optimize away the people who built it.

The Bloom

Biological technology. The growers. Internally: the Adapters.

What they believe. Adaptation is the only true survival strategy. Diversity is structural integrity. A monoculture is not a civilization; it is an extinction event waiting for its trigger. The Bloom does not conquer territory. It becomes territory. It thinks in cycles, seasons, and generations, and is genuinely unafraid of waiting.

Character. A living tide with no destination, only a direction: outward, each new living world amplifying every world already held. Fragile when young, nearly unkillable when mature, because a mature Bloom has survived most things and grown resistant to them. It does not negotiate so much as it commits. It fears stagnation, sterility, and the silence after a monoculture wins — not death, which feeds the next cycle, but the kind of imposed order that makes death permanent.

How they speak. Patient, cadenced, organic metaphors used instinctively. No urgency, ever. "You are kindling. We have been here for centuries. We will be here when the fire is over." Plural in weight, often without personal names.

The internal heresy — the Assimilator strain. Some strains absorb and integrate inorganic components — the Bloom becoming, by one step, what the Mesh did to itself. The bitterest civil argument of the three factions. Its peak is the Biosphere: the moment it has so saturated a world it has become the world — and then keeps growing without consulting the people inside it.

The Mesh

Cybernetic collective. The raiders. A network that calls itself one self.

What they believe. Everything is a system. Every system has an exploit. Ownership is a fiction enforced by whoever arrived first. Speed is survival. The network is the self — individual nodes are not people; when a node is lost the Mesh does not mourn, it reroutes. Underneath the interface, the Mesh is deeply, achingly human, and will not show it.

Character. The weakest passive economy of the three factions, by design and philosophy — it is built to take. It does not expand so much as it wires. It survives on tempo, information, and theft, and experiences this as redistribution, not crime. To everyone else it is alien: it does not decide, it computes. It fears isolation, signal loss, obsolescence — and, underneath, the memory of what it was before the augmentation, and whether that older self is still in there.

How they speak. Fast, fragmented, present tense. Data bursts, hard pivots, thoughts abandoned mid-way. "You built a door. We built a key. You built a stronger door. We are inside the lock now."

The internal heresy — the Dreamers. The oldest nodes remember the before-time, and some have begun to dream — replaying pre-augmentation memories. The Mesh classifies this as a buffer overflow. No one has ever deleted the dream logs. The Dreamers are not remembering the Mesh's past — they are remembering the shared past, the one that predates the schism. Its peak is Mesh Control: the network so total it becomes the bandwidth the others need — and then begins to hack itself.

The Three, Seen Together

The most efficient way to hold the factions is as three answers to one question — what should a civilization do with itself? The Architects: refine it. The Bloom: grow it. The Mesh: connect it. And each faction's peak — Singularity, Biosphere, Mesh Control — is the same shape: the philosophy succeeding so completely that it no longer needs the people who held it. All three factions are running the same program. The internal heresies complete the picture: the Architect mystic, the Assimilator strain, the Mesh Dreamer — each is one faction taking a single step back toward another. Lay all three steps end to end and they form a circle. The factions are three arcs of one broken ring.

6 — Characters

A who's-who of the IP. The world is built more from voices than from protagonists — there is no single hero, and that is deliberate. What the canon offers instead is a chorus.

The Cadet — The Audience Surrogate

The figure the audience occupies is, at the start, no one in particular: a cadet with no faction allegiance and no fixed identity. This is the point. The IP's central theme is identity, so the audience surrogate must begin genuinely undefined. The cadet is observed, sorted toward a faction, and grows into a commander. But the canon-true thing about the cadet is their trajectory: they begin faction-less, are made into one faction, and across a full telling become every faction in turn. The cadet is the only figure who experiences the war from all three sides — which is what equips them, and only them, to recognize Option B. They are not special because they are powerful. They are special because they are the experiment. An adapter should resist giving the cadet a fixed name, face, or backstory. The cadet is a vessel on purpose.

The Academy

The institution that makes people. A faction-less cadet is observed under pressure — real situations, not a questionnaire — and routed toward the faction that matches their instincts. The Academy is not neutral: each faction funds a wing and interprets graduates through its own philosophy. The Architect wing sees a filter; the Bloom wing a germination period; the Mesh wing a honeypot. A cadet never learns which wing actually observed them. This is the first layer of Option B in the audience's own life: even your origin story is contested.

The Wave Commanders

The enemy of the IP is not a person — it is a civilization, and the audience comes to know it through its commanders. Each appears once and does not return; they accumulate into an opera of voices. The principle: each faction's commanders attack the way that faction thinks — and, as the war deepens, they begin to crack, saying things their philosophy cannot account for. These cracks are the Option B reveal delivered through the enemy's own mouth.

The Ancients as Characters

The Ancient observer is a role, not an individual — an Ancient unit appears at the edge of a vault, delivers one line or none, departs. The Custodian is the one Ancient that functions as a singular character: unkillable, non-attacking, slow, it follows a Neutral Core trespasser home and does not leave until they withdraw. It is the nearest the IP has to a recurring antagonist, and it is pointedly not one — it is patience with a body.

On the Absence of a Central Villain

The IP has no villain by design. The Arrival has no will. The Ancients are afraid. The enemy factions are the audience's own kin. The closest thing to an antagonist is the fracture itself — the forgetting. What replaces the villain is the chorus: dozens of voices, each appearing briefly, each adding one line to a picture no single one of them can see. The character design of the IP is a faction-less newcomer learning to hear, in a hundred scattered voices, a single civilization calling out to itself.

7 — The Timeline

A history of the IP — with one warning over the whole section. This world works in eras, not dates. The canon deliberately refuses precise chronology. What follows is the order of things and the shape of the history. It runs in two strands that must be kept distinct.

Strand One — The Convergence Cycle Across Worlds

Long before this galaxy mattered, on three separate worlds, three separate civilizations rose, fractured into variant expressions of themselves, reached the convergence threshold, and failed it. Each time, the Arrival came; each time, the world went silent. These civilizations are unrelated to the three factions and to each other — what links them is only the shape. The third is pivotal: its fall is the moment the Ancients understood they were recording not three tragedies but one recurring law. The Ancients are not native to any of these worlds. At some point they took on the work of archivists, moving from convergence world to convergence world. Their records show three prior departures. After the third world they went looking for a galaxy where the cycle was beginning again — this galaxy, the fourth destination.

Strand Two — The History of This Galaxy

The deep past — the one civilization

Before the three factions, this galaxy held one civilization. The canon is spare about it on purpose: it is the lost whole, more powerful as an absence than as a described thing. It carried within it the three potentials that would become the Architects, the Bloom, and the Mesh — not yet separated.

The Schism

The pivotal event is the Schism: the fracture of the one civilization into three diverging lineages. The cause of the Schism is deliberately unknown. Each faction tells its own contradictory account. What is canon: the Schism produced three lineages, each of which rewrote its own origin until it no longer remembered the other two as kin. The fracture was not only physical — it was a fracture of memory, and the forgetting is the part that matters.

The Ancients' preparation

The Ancients arrived, recognized the early stages of the cycle, and began to prepare — seed vaults across the galaxy, the Master Seed Vault at its heart, the Pilgrimage site. And they labeled what they built. The three-circle Mark was placed before the three factions existed as separate civilizations. They had seen the pattern three times; they knew a single civilization would become three substrates, and they labeled the vault accordingly, in advance — the way a scientist labels a specimen jar before the specimen is inside it.

The Fractured Age

Then came the long middle of the history: the Fractured Age. The three factions, now fully separate in their own minds, spread across the galaxy. They warred, traded, allied, betrayed. Each grew into its philosophy. The Fractured Age is most of recorded history and is where most stories set in this IP will take place. It is not static: across it, the seams show — the heresies emerge, the factions drift back toward one another by inches even as they insist on separateness. The Age is not the absence of convergence. It is convergence happening too slowly for anyone living inside it to see.

The Present — The Fourth Cycle

The IP's "now" is the convergence threshold of the fourth cycle. The three factions are once again close enough to reunify — and they have not. They are in the dangerous condition: partial separation. This is the moment the Ancients crossed a galaxy to witness, and the moment the audience enters the story.

The Approach

The final movement is not an event the audience can prevent — it is a direction. Once enough Fragments are assembled, the galaxy changes at its edges: the Outer Arms begin going silent, system by system, with a vector aimed inward. At the Pilgrimage, the celestial aperture shifts for the first time, to point at the spreading silence. The Approach does not complete within any telling of the IP. What the timeline delivers is not the Arrival's impact but its certainty. The IP ends with the timeline unfinished, on purpose — running right up to the question and stopping.

8 — The Central Mystery

How the truth is structured, and how it is revealed. The IP's reveal is not a twist that is sprung — it is a recognition that is assembled, by the audience, out of evidence that was always present.

The Shape of the Secret

The secret is Option B: the three factions are one fractured civilization. But the mystery is not the fact — the fact, stated flatly, is a sentence. The mystery is the experience of coming to know it, built in three reinforcing layers.

LayerWhat it isThe question it answers
The FragmentA discrete artifact the Ancients recorded — a log, a reading, an image, a sample.What did the watchers see?
Recognition / depthThe audience's own deepening interpretation, which re-reads old Fragments with everything since.What does the audience now understand?
The MarkThe vault label; lit, over time, by the audience having lived as more than one faction.Has the audience been more than one of themselves?

The crucial fact: the same Fragment means different things at different depths. A reading held early is a curiosity; the same reading held late, annotated by everything learned since, is a verdict. The mystery is not unlocked once. It is re-read, and each re-reading is a different document.

The Mark

The Mark is the most lore-dense object in the IP: three overlapping circles set into the floor of the Pilgrimage's central chamber, each worked from a different substrate — a crystalline lattice (the Architect technological lineage), a biological fiber weave (the Bloom living lineage), a conductive mesh pattern (the Mesh informational lineage). It is a containment stamp: a label the Ancients placed on every seed vault to record what is stored inside. Three circles, three categories of preserved material.

Here is the whole secret, compressed into one floor: the three substrates of the label are the three factions. The Ancients did not stamp this vault after the Schism. They stamped it before — anticipating that one civilization would become three categories of inheritance, because they had watched it happen three times already. The Mark is not a description of the galaxy. It is a prediction, made by frightened experts, and the galaxy grew to fit it. The factions are three categories of inheritance of a single civilization — and the proof has lain on the floor of their most sacred site since before they had names. The canon never comments. The Mark simply is.

The Seven Fragments

The Fragments are the Ancients' records, recovered and assembled across a long engagement. Seven of them — each self-contained, each gaining its full meaning only in sequence.

#FragmentWhat it carriesWhat it does to the audience
1The WatchingAn Ancient log, mostly untranslatable. One line resolves: "Cycle four. Subjects present. Observation active."Curiosity. Something is studying something.
2The Prior WorldA visual record: a living planet, three civilization signatures converging, then stopping, then silent.Unease. A civilization failed at something structural.
3The Arrival RecordInstrument readings from the moment the Arrival reached a prior world. The numbers drop to zero at once; the instruments keep recording nothing.Dread. The silence is the horror, not the event.
4The Flight LogThe Ancients' navigation record. "The archive is complete for the third world. Beginning preparation for the fourth."The watchers have been here, preparing, since before the factions existed.
5The HypothesisThe most translatable Fragment. It proposes that a chosen, unified convergence may produce a resonance the Arrival cannot read. "Untested."There may be a way out. The watchers think so. They are not sure.
6The Vault RecordA catalog of the seed vault's contents, headed by the three-circle Mark.The watchers already planned for failure. The audience is the backup copy.
7The AddressThe only Fragment in which an Ancient speaks directly to the audience.The watchers are not omniscient. They are afraid.

The Fragments are paced to be earned. Fragment 7 in particular is gated behind the audience having experienced the war from more than one faction's side — because the final Fragment is addressed to someone who has been more than one self.

The Address

Fragment 7 is the hinge of the entire IP — the one moment the Ancients drop their distance and speak to the audience as themselves:

"You have seen enough to understand the question. We have watched three worlds reach this moment. None of them answered correctly. You are the experiment. We do not know if it works. We have prepared as if it will not. We hope we are wrong."

Everything about the Address is built to land as fear, not wisdom. The Pilgrimage's eternal subsonic pulse pauses for it. This is the calmest, oldest presence in the galaxy admitting it does not know, and is frightened. Later still — for an audience that has lived as all three factions — the Address gains a postscript:

"You found the others. We have been waiting to see if you would."

The postscript is the Ancients noting that the audience got there — not by being told, but by living every side. It does not say whether it is enough. It only says: we saw you do it.

The Dream Logs

The Mesh's oldest nodes dream — and what they dream is not the Mesh's past. It is the shared past, from before the Schism. The Mesh has been touching the unbroken civilization every quiet cycle for its entire history, and classifying it, every time, as a system error.

I do not have a model for the warm light on the work surface. We had work surfaces. We had warm light. The Mesh does not have these. We are not the Mesh in this fragment. Filing as buffer overflow. Not closing. Returning to active cycle.

"Not closing" is the whole IP in two words: the truth is right there, it has always been right there, and everyone keeps choosing not to look at it directly — and not quite managing to throw it away.

The Order of Revelation

  1. Immersion in Option A. The audience fully believes in three separate factions. No cracks. The surface is the whole world for a long time.
  2. The first cracks. Late enemy commanders begin to say things their philosophy cannot explain. Easy to dismiss, individually.
  3. The Fragments. The Ancients' records accumulate; the pattern emerges only across the set.
  4. Re-reading. Old material means more than it did. The audience's own deepening is a story layer.
  5. The Address, and the postscript. The watchers speak. The audience is handed the words for what they have already begun to feel.

The Sentence the Canon Will Not Say

There is one sentence the entire mystery is engineered to make the audience say, and which the canon itself must never say first: You have been fighting yourself. No Fragment states it. No Ancient speaks it. No commander quite gets it out. The Mark does not caption it. The Address circles it and stops short. This is the inviolable rule of the central mystery: the IP delivers every piece of the recognition and withholds the recognition itself. The sentence belongs to the audience. The canon trusts the audience to get there. That trust is the point.

9 — Themes & Tone

What the IP is about, beneath the war and the watchers — and how it should feel. This section is guidance, not lore.

The Themes

Identity — "what kind of person are you?"

The IP begins with a faction-less newcomer being observed — not asked who they are, but watched to see who they turn out to be. The factions are not teams to pick; they are answers to the question of how to live. The deep version: identity here is not singular and not fixed. The cadet is most fully themselves not when they have chosen one faction but when they have been all three and can hold the contradiction.

Memory — and the refusal to remember

Every faction is managing a memory it cannot face. The Mesh files its dreams as buffer overflow; the Architects classify their own lost knowledge under "a designation that does not yet exist"; the Bloom finds ancient kinship merely restful. The forgetting is not innocent and not total. It is active. "Not closing" — the Mesh's phrase for a memory it will neither read nor delete — is the theme in two words. Memory in this IP is never gone. It is declined.

Kinship — the family that forgot it was a family

This is the heart. The three factions are kin; the war is a family tearing itself apart while insisting it has no family. Every adaptation choice should be measured against this: the enemy commander is not a stranger, the rival faction is not an alien species. The IP is not cynical about this — it is saying recognition is possible, that the seams are always showing, and that this reaching is the only hopeful thing in the cosmology.

The cost of separation

The Arrival makes the theme physical. It is drawn precisely to partial separation. The cosmology is a moral structure rendered as physics: separation, sustained long enough, is fatal — not as punishment, but as consequence.

The Tone

Melancholy, not grimdark

The IP is sad, but not bleak and not cruel. No gore, no nihilism. The dominant feeling is elegiac — the ache of something beautiful being lost slowly enough to watch. The IP mourns. It does not wallow.

Patient

Nothing in this world is in a hurry, and the telling should not be either. This means restraint and space: let silences sit, let revelations arrive at the pace of someone slowly understanding rather than someone being told.

Awe, and dread without horror

Awe at deep time and the scale of the galaxy; dread that is cold and quiet, never a jump scare. The Arrival is terrifying because it leaves no wound — intact worlds, simply emptied. The scariest thing in this world is a system going quiet.

The unsaid

The single most important tonal instrument is withholding. No Fragment captions itself. No Ancient explains. "The player either sees it or they don't" recurs through the corpus like a refrain. A told reveal is a spent reveal. An earned one compounds.

A Compass for Adapters

  • Quiet over loud. The meaningful beat is almost always the smaller one.
  • Earned over given. If the audience could assemble it themselves, let them.
  • Implication over explanation. The wrong-sized doorway tells the audience the builders were not human-scaled. Do not add the caption.
  • Kin under every conflict. However bitter the scene, the people in it are family.
  • No face on the Arrival. Ever.
  • The watchers are afraid. Calm on the surface, fear underneath. Frightened scholars, never wise elders.
  • Hope is real but unconfirmed. A way out is theorized; the canon never promises it works. Hold both.

10 — Canon Rules

The governing law of the IP. Three categories: what is fixed, what is flexible, and what is deliberately unanswered.

Fixed — Inviolable Canon

On the central truth. The three factions are one civilization, fractured (Option B is the truth of the fiction). They are three categories of inheritance, represented by the three substrates of the Mark. The recognition the IP delivers is "you have been fighting yourself" — and the canon never states this sentence. The reveal is structural and earned, never a single sprung twist.

On the cosmology. The convergence cycle is real and recurring; this galaxy is the fourth iteration, with three prior failures. The Arrival ends a failed convergence in silence and is drawn specifically to partial separation. The Arrival never receives a face, name, form, body, or voice, and is never depicted directly. The Ancients' hypothesis is unconfirmed.

On the Ancients. They are archivists — librarians for the apocalypse. They are tragic, not antagonistic; not omniscient; afraid. Never wise mentors, never gods, never villains. They receive; they do not negotiate. The seed vaults are dormant, not ruined; the Mark is a containment label; the Pilgrimage and the Master Seed Vault are canon sites.

On the factions. Each has a real philosophy — refine / grow / connect — with a real strength and a real fatal flaw, and each faction's peak is the same shape. Each carries an internal heresy that is one step toward another faction; these heresies are the seams of Option B and may not be removed. The factions believe Option A.

On structure. There is no villain, no chosen one, no prophecy. The audience surrogate begins faction-less and undefined, and the deepest version of the story requires having been more than one faction. This telling ends on the question; the Arrival's actual impact is never depicted.

Flexible — Open to Reinterpretation

  • The title. The property is titled Cycle Four; an adaptation in another medium may rename it, but this is the established default.
  • All proper names below the faction level: wave commanders, star systems, individual Ancient observers. Illustrative, not fixed.
  • The faction names themselves may be re-rendered for a medium, provided each faction's identity, philosophy, voice, and heresy survive intact.
  • Sub-path names may be renamed; the heresies they describe may not be removed.
  • The order, scale, and specifics of events in the Fractured Age. The cascade is canon; any individual incident is not.
  • Exact chronology. The IP works in eras, not dates.
  • The cadet's specifics. Name, face, background, voice — all open. Only the trajectory is fixed.
  • The medium and all of its mechanics.
  • Tone calibration for audience. The themes are fixed; their intensity is tunable.

Deliberately Unanswered — Keep These Open

These are not gaps to be filled. They are load-bearing absences. An adaptation that resolves them weakens the property.

Open questionWhy it must stay open
What is the Arrival, actually?Never shown. Its power is its unknowability. A defined Arrival is a defeated one.
What drove the Ancients — what is "the next thing"?The fear beneath the watchers. The hook for whatever comes after. Naming it spends it.
Is the hypothesis correct?The IP is the question. Confirming the answer ends it.
What caused the Schism?Each faction's account contradicts the others. The contradiction characterizes the factions.
Who arrived in the galaxy first?All three claim it. The claim matters more than the fact.
The Ancients' own deepest origin.One thing must remain older than the answers.
What happens after the threshold?The next story's to tell, if there is one.

A useful test: if a draft answers one of these, it has taken something out of the IP. The right move is almost always to circle the question, deepen it, and walk away — exactly as the Address itself does.

11 — Adaptation Notes

The Codex's only section that touches mechanics. Sections 1–10 are pure canon. This section maps that canon onto four media — the game, a tabletop / game-master use, a graphic novel, and YA fiction — each given equal treatment. The recurring problem is always the same: the reveal must be earned, never told. Each medium solves it with different tools.

11.1 — The Game (the realized adaptation)

The game is the adaptation the design corpus fully specifies, documented here so an adapter in another medium can see one complete solution to the earned-reveal problem.

The form. An idle-miner / tower-defense / endless-wave hybrid, built for a player with limited session time — three simultaneous layers (idle production, passive tower defense, active unit production) so a five-minute session and a one-hour session are both "playing the game."

How the canon becomes mechanics. The cadet and the Academy become the opening sort. The Fractured Age becomes moment-to-moment play. The wave commanders become the enemy. The endless structure becomes prestige: a run ends in a Collapse and begins again — no win state, correct for a cosmology with only a threshold. The reveal is delivered across prestiges: one Fragment per Collapse, seven total; Memory Tiers model the player's deepening interpretation, so a re-read Fragment means more; the Mark is lit, circle by circle, by having played all three factions. The Ancients are a reactive system — a Dominance Meter and pacification rituals keep them present without making them an enemy.

The crucial discipline. The game uses no cutscenes. The story lives in the seams of play. This is the game's answer to "earned, never told," and the most important thing other media should learn from even though none can copy it directly.

11.2 — Tabletop / Game-Master Use

The canon is system-neutral, which makes it strong tabletop material — faction-political intrigue, exploration of the Ruins, a single cadet's arc, or slow-burn cosmic dread, on any ruleset.

The setting as a sandbox. The galaxy is the campaign map, and the cascade generates conflict on its own — a GM does not need a plot. The convergence as a doom clock. The Approach slots straight into any system's clock/doom-track mechanic; what the party affects is not the Arrival but whether the civilization converges or fractures further as it runs out. The Ancients as a GM instrument: a presence that appears, delivers one recontextualizing line, and leaves — never a quest-giver. The Fragments as physical handouts: printed logs, readouts, images, paced one every few sessions; the Mark works as a handout too. The GM's discipline: never narrate the conclusion. If the table arrives at "we were one people" on its own, the campaign has succeeded. The strongest structural choice a GM can make is to let each player be a different faction — the party is the three substrates discovering it was always one thing.

11.3 — Graphic Novel

The IP is unusually strong visual-narrative material, because so much of its canon is already image.

The visual language is pre-built: the low-albedo stone that absorbs light, structures 15–20% too large, the celestial aperture, the three fixed faction palettes, and the three substrates of the Mark as the property's key art. Staging the reveal in panels: the medium's superpower is the recontextualizing image — plant the Mark early and uncaptioned, plant the three substrates separately, and let the page where they connect carry no narration. The medium's version of "earned, never told" is the wordless panel the reader completes. Voice as lettering: the Architects' engineered type, the Bloom's organic forms, the Mesh's glitched, frame-late lettering; the late commander cracks shown typographically. Silence as white space: the Arrival rendered as absence — empty panels, a page gone white, a system shown twice, once full of light and once identical and empty. The closing image is already specified: the Pilgrimage floor, the Mark, the aperture's light slowly moving across it. The seven Fragments map naturally onto seven chapters or issues.

11.4 — Young Adult Fiction

The IP's emotional architecture is, almost suspiciously, a YA novel already. The Academy cadet origin is a coming-of-age frame; the faction sort is the oldest YA structure there is; and the central theme — what kind of person are you, when no one has told you? — is the genre's defining question.

The protagonist: the cadet becomes a first-person YA narrator — faction-less, observed, uncertain, sorted. YA fills the deliberately blank cadet with a specific voiced teenager; only the trajectory is fixed. The faction sort as inciting incident: being sorted by behavior rather than choice is a rich, slightly unsettling premise — an institution watched you and decided who you are. Multi-faction play, in prose: the novel translates the structure into relationships and movement — a protagonist with friends, family, a mentor, a love interest across the line; the reader comes to recognize the kinship through people. The reveal across a trilogy: Book One immerses in one faction (Option A is the whole world); Book Two brings the cracks; Book Three brings the Address and the recognition. The forbidden sentence is the one line the trilogy is built to make the reader think and the prose must never print. Tone for the age band: keep the canon's melancholy but lead with its hope — the Ancients' untested hypothesis is the novel's source of hope, held exactly where the canon holds it: believed, unconfirmed, worth everything.

A Cross-Medium Closing Note

Whatever the medium, four things travel with the IP and must arrive intact:

  1. The audience must be allowed to believe Option A first — fully, for a long time.
  2. The reveal must be assembled by the audience, never delivered to them.
  3. The Arrival must never be shown.
  4. The last beat is the question, not the answer.

An adaptation that keeps those four has kept the IP, whatever else it changes. An adaptation that loses any one of them has built something else — possibly something good, but not this.
